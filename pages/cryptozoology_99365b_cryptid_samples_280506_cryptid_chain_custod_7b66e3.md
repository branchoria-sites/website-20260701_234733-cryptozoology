## Introduction

A cryptid sample can reach a reputable laboratory and still fail as persuasive evidence if nobody can demonstrate where it came from, who handled it, or whether it remained unchanged along the way. In [cryptozoology]({{'cryptozoology/' | relative_url }}), this problem is especially important because alleged hair, tissue, bone and skin samples are often collected outside formal scientific fieldwork. A laboratory may be able to identify the species represented by a specimen, but if the specimen’s history is poorly documented, the result cannot be confidently linked to the claimed encounter, [location]({{'location/' | relative_url }}) or creature. In forensic science, this documented history is known as the chain of custody: the record of collection, transfer, handling and storage from discovery to analysis. Without it, even a scientifically testable sample can lose much of its evidential value.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nist.gov/glossary-term/20076" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nist.gov">[NIST+2NIST Computer Security Resource Center]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nist.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">glossary term</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Chain Of Custody &#124; NIST6 Apr 2026 — Chain Of Custody. chronological record (3.49) of the transfer, handling... ## Why Provenance Matters Before Testing

For cryptid investigations, the key question is not merely “What is this material?” but also “How do we know it came from the claimed source?” A strand of hair identified through DNA testing may genuinely belong to a bear, a human or another animal. However, if the sample passed through multiple collectors, sat for years in an unlabelled container, or was exchanged among enthusiasts without records, the laboratory result cannot reliably answer questions about the original sighting.

This distinction explains why forensic standards place heavy emphasis on tracking evidence from collection through storage and analysis. Chain-of-custody records exist to demonstrate that a specimen examined in the laboratory is the same specimen originally collected in the field and that it was not contaminated, substituted or mixed with other material during handling. In cryptozoology, [provenance]({{'provenance/' | relative_url }}) problems are common because many [specimens]({{'specimens/' | relative_url }}) originate from private collections, museums, expeditions, anonymous submissions or local traditions rather than controlled scientific surveys. The longer and less documented the journey, the harder it becomes to connect a biological result to a cryptid claim.

## Common Handoff and Storage Failures

### Unrecorded Transfers

One of the most damaging mistakes is failing to document who possessed a sample at each stage. A collector may hand material to a researcher, who sends it to another investigator, who later forwards it to a laboratory. If dates, names and transfer [conditions]({{'conditions/' | relative_url }}) are missing, there is no reliable record showing continuous control of the specimen.

In forensic practice, every transfer should be documented because unexplained gaps create opportunities for accidental mix-ups or deliberate substitution. When such gaps exist, confidence in the sample's origin declines sharply.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK551677/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[NCBI+2Iris Unipa]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">NCBIChain of Custody</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">It is necessary to assure the court of law that the evidence is authentic, ie...</span></span></span>

### Vague Collection Information

Many alleged cryptid specimens are accompanied by stories rather than documentation. A label such as “found near a remote mountain trail” is far weaker than a record containing exact location data, collection date, environmental conditions and collector identity.

A sample without a precise collection context may still yield a DNA result, but that result cannot be firmly tied to a specific event or encounter. ### Mixing Samples from Different Sources

Another recurring problem occurs when multiple hairs, tissue fragments or biological traces are stored together. Once specimens become mixed, investigators may no longer know which fragment originated from which location or event.

This risk is especially serious when only a small amount of material survives. A laboratory may successfully sequence DNA from a mixed collection, yet the resulting identification may correspond to a contaminating specimen rather than the material originally thought to be anomalous.

### Improper Packaging

Biological evidence can degrade when packaged incorrectly. Forensic guidance stresses that storage conditions must preserve the specimen and minimise [contamination]({{'contamination/' | relative_url }}). Moist biological material sealed for extended periods in unsuitable packaging can promote degradation and microbial growth, potentially damaging DNA. ### Informal Handling by Multiple People

A specimen passed around among collectors, expedition members, journalists or curiosity seekers accumulates opportunities for contamination. Human skin cells, hair, saliva and environmental material can be introduced unintentionally.

Modern DNA methods are sensitive enough to detect extremely small quantities of genetic material. Consequently, contamination concerns are not limited to dramatic mishandling; repeated casual contact can be enough to complicate later analysis.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://knowledge.lancashire.ac.uk/id/eprint/46224/1/Maintaining%20the%20Chain%20of%20Custody%20Anti-Contamination%20Measures%20for%20Trace%20DNA%20Evidence.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: knowledge.lancashire.ac.uk">[UCLan - University of Central Lancashire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">knowledge.lancashire.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University of Central LancashireAnti-contamination measures for trace DNA evidenceby SK Alketbi · 2023 · Cited by 35 — The chain of custo...</span></span></span>

## When Laboratory Results Cannot Rescue Poor Custody

A common misunderstanding is that advanced DNA testing can compensate for weak provenance. In reality, testing can identify material only after it arrives at the laboratory. It cannot reconstruct an undocumented history.

Suppose a tissue sample submitted as evidence of an unknown primate is identified as bear tissue. The result may be accurate, but without reliable custody records it remains unclear whether the analysed tissue was genuinely connected to the original claim. ## What a Credible Transfer Record Includes

A strong custody record does not require a police investigation, but it does require disciplined documentation. Key elements include:

* The collector's identity and contact information.
* The exact location and date of collection.
* Photographs showing the specimen in its original context.
* Notes describing collection methods and environmental conditions.
* Unique labels linking the specimen to field records.
* Documentation of every transfer between individuals or organisations.
* Records of storage conditions and any opening of containers.
* Documentation of laboratory receipt and analysis. ## The Point at Which a Sample Loses Its Story

A cryptid sample does not become weak evidence solely because it turns out to belong to a known animal. It becomes weak evidence when investigators can no longer demonstrate the connection between the biological material and the event, creature or location being claimed.

The central challenge is therefore not only obtaining hair, tissue or other biological traces. It is preserving their story. Once documentation gaps appear, transfers go unrecorded, storage conditions become uncertain or contamination cannot be excluded, the specimen may still be testable in a laboratory, but its ability to support an extraordinary zoological claim is greatly diminished.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nist.gov/glossary-term/20076" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nist.gov">[NIST+2NIST]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nist.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">glossary term</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Chain Of Custody &#124; NIST6 Apr 2026 — Chain Of Custody. chronological record (3.49) of the transfer, handling...
