## Introduction

Among the most tangible pieces of Yeti evidence were not [footprints]({{'footprints/' | relative_url }}) or eyewitness reports but physical relics preserved in monasteries, caves, museums and private collections. For decades, these objects were displayed as possible remains of the legendary Himalayan creature: patches of skin, hair bundles, bones, teeth and even parts of hands or paws. Modern genetic testing has transformed the discussion. Instead of revealing an unknown primate, the strongest analyses have repeatedly identified known animals, especially Himalayan and Tibetan bears. In one notable case, a supposed Yeti tooth proved to belong to a domestic dog. These findings did not simply dismiss [folklore]({{'folklore/' | relative_url }}); they showed how ordinary animal remains could acquire extraordinary identities as they moved through religious, cultural and expeditionary contexts.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalgeographic.com/science/article/yeti-legends-real-animals-dna-bears-himalaya-science" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalgeographic.com">[National Geographic]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalgeographic.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">yeti legends real animals dna bears himalaya science</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National GeographicYeti Legends Are Based on These Real Animals, DNA...29 Nov 2017 — DNA analysis of multiple supposed Yeti samples—incl...</span></span></span><img src="{{"/assets/images/cryptozoology_99365b_yeti_bear_dna_3a5a98_yeti_relic_identitie_7df5b7-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Relics illustration 1" data-theme-src-dark="{{"/assets/images/cryptozoology_99365b_yeti_bear_dna_3a5a98_yeti_relic_identitie_7df5b7-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{"/assets/images/cryptozoology_99365b_yeti_bear_dna_3a5a98_yeti_relic_identitie_7df5b7-Illustration-1-light.svg" | relative_url }}" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">
## The paw, skin, femur, hair and tooth examples

The most influential study of [Yeti relics]({{'yeti-relics/' | relative_url }}) was published in 2017 by a team led by Charlotte Lindqvist. Researchers examined nine [specimens]({{'specimens/' | relative_url }}) attributed to the Yeti, including bone, skin, hair, tooth and faecal material gathered from museums, private collections and Himalayan sources. Eight were identified as bears and one as a dog.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.buffalo.edu/ubnow/stories/2017/11/lindqvist-yeti-bears.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: buffalo.edu">[University at Buffalo+2National Geographic]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">buffalo.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">lindqvist yeti bears</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University at BuffaloStudy ties DNA samples from purported yetis to Asian bears29 Nov 2017 — A new study led by UB biologist Charlotte Li...</span></span></span>

## How monastery relics acquired a Yeti identity

Many famous Yeti relics originated in Buddhist monasteries rather than scientific collections. The objects were often embedded in local stories, pilgrimage traditions and sacred histories long before Western explorers became interested in them.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://explorehimalaya.com/yeti-myth-mystery-sacred-legends-himalaya/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: explorehimalaya.com">[explorehimalaya.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">explorehimalaya.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Yeti: Myth, Mystery, and Sacred Legends of the HimalayaOctober 29, 2025 — Here stands the 340-year-old Khumjung Monastery, a place of...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 29, 2025</span></span></span>

The well-known scalp preserved at Khumjung Monastery illustrates this process. The relic became internationally famous after expeditions arranged examinations by zoologists and anatomists. Investigators generally concluded that it was not evidence of a Yeti, yet the object remained important within its local cultural setting. Its significance derived not only from biological identity but also from its connection to local history and religious tradition.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.atlasobscura.com/places/the-yeti-scalp-of-khumjung-khumjung-nepal" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: atlasobscura.com">[Atlas Obscura]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">atlasobscura.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Atlas Obscura The Yeti Scalp of Khumjung</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Atlas ObscuraThe Yeti Scalp of KhumjungMarch 23, 2017 — 23 Mar 2017 — A small village monastery in northeast Nepal claims to have a 300-y...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 23, 2017</span></span></span>

A similar pattern surrounded relics associated with Pangboche Monastery. Stories linked the objects to revered religious figures and long-standing local traditions. Later scientific examination of some associated remains failed to support a Yeti origin, but this did not erase the cultural narratives that had developed around them.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pangboche_Hand" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pangboche Hand</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Pangboche Hand</span></span></span>

## Why relic identity can survive without fraud

A common misunderstanding is that a bear identification automatically implies deliberate deception. The evidence does not require that conclusion.

Many relics entered collections before modern zoology, genetics or wildlife surveys were available in remote Himalayan regions. A bear skull fragment, patch of skin or unusual bone found under mysterious circumstances could plausibly be interpreted through local traditions that already included stories of wild, human-like mountain beings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.buffalo.edu/ubnow/stories/2017/11/lindqvist-yeti-bears.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: buffalo.edu">[University at Buffalo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">buffalo.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">lindqvist yeti bears</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University at BuffaloStudy ties DNA samples from purported yetis to Asian bears29 Nov 2017 — A new study led by UB biologist Charlotte Li...</span></span></span>

* Bears can leave [tracks]({{'tracks/' | relative_url }}) that appear surprisingly human-like in snow, especially when hind feet overlap front footprints.<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Himalayan brown bears are large, uncommon and often encountered only briefly in difficult terrain.

## What DNA can and cannot say about local tradition

Genetic analysis is exceptionally good at answering one question: which species provided the sample? For many Yeti relics, the answer is now clear. The tested specimens overwhelmingly trace back to Himalayan brown bears, Tibetan brown bears, Asian black bears and, in one case, a dog.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.buffalo.edu/ubnow/stories/2017/11/lindqvist-yeti-bears.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: buffalo.edu">[University at Buffalo+2National Geographic]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">buffalo.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">lindqvist yeti bears</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University at BuffaloStudy ties DNA samples from purported yetis to Asian bears29 Nov 2017 — A new study led by UB biologist Charlotte Li...</span></span></span>

What DNA cannot determine is why communities interpreted those remains as Yeti relics, how the associated stories evolved, or what social and religious functions the objects served.
