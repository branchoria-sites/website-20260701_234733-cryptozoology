## Introduction

The Oxford–Lausanne hair study is one of the most important laboratory investigations ever conducted into alleged cryptid biological evidence. Published in 2014 and led by geneticist Bryan Sykes, the project sought to move discussion of creatures such as the yeti, Bigfoot, sasquatch and almasty away from anecdotes and towards testable physical samples. Instead of investigating sightings or [footprints]({{'footprints/' | relative_url }}), the researchers focused on hair [specimens]({{'specimens/' | relative_url }}) that collectors, museums and enthusiasts believed might have come from unknown primates. The study's significance lies not in confirming a cryptid, but in showing what happens when purported cryptid evidence is subjected to modern genetic analysis. Of the 30 hair samples successfully tested, almost all were identified as known animals, while two Himalayan samples produced an unexpected result that sparked years of scientific debate.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://royalsocietypublishing.org/rspb/article/281/1789/20140161/77194/Genetic-analysis-of-hair-samples-attributed-to" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: royalsocietypublishing.org">[Royal Society Publishing+2PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">royalsocietypublishing.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Sykes Corresponding Author · Bryan C. ## How the sample call-out worked

The project began with a public appeal for biological material linked to reports of anomalous primates. Researchers associated with the University of Oxford and the Cantonal Museum of Zoology in Lausanne invited submissions from around the world, including museum specimens, private collections and material gathered during cryptid investigations. The aim was not to prove or disprove any specific creature but to create a systematic survey of available evidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hurriyetdailynews.com/yetis-hair-samples-looked-for-a-study-21518" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hurriyetdailynews.com">[Hürriyet Daily News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hurriyetdailynews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">yetis hair samples looked for a study 21518</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Hürriyet Daily NewsYeti&#x27;s hair samples looked for a study24 May 2012 — Bryan Sykes of Oxford University said the group had already receiv...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 2012</span></span></span>

A total of 57 [samples]({{'samples/' | relative_url }}) were initially submitted. Many could not be analysed because they lacked sufficient material or failed quality checks. Thirty samples ultimately yielded DNA suitable for identification. The researchers applied decontamination procedures designed to remove surface [contamination]({{'contamination/' | relative_url }}) and then sequenced a short region of mitochondrial DNA known as the 12S ribosomal RNA gene. This genetic marker is particularly useful when dealing with old, degraded hair because it often survives when longer DNA sequences do not. The resulting sequences were compared against reference databases to determine the most likely species source.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4100498/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Genetic analysis of hair samples attributed to yeti, bigfoot and...by BC Sykes · 2014 · Cited by 49 — We have used rigorous decontami...</span></span></span>

## What the 30 tested hairs matched

The headline finding was straightforward: none of the analysed samples provided evidence for an unknown primate species. ## Why the polar bear result became controversial

Two samples stood apart from all the others. One came from Ladakh in northern India and the other from Bhutan. Both had been associated with local yeti traditions. When the researchers compared the DNA sequences with available databases, they found a close match not to modern brown bears but to DNA from a fossil polar bear jawbone dating to the Pleistocene.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4100498/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Genetic analysis of hair samples attributed to yeti, bigfoot and...by BC Sykes · 2014 · Cited by 49 — We have used rigorous decontami...</span></span></span>

The finding attracted enormous media attention because it appeared to suggest either an unusual bear lineage or perhaps a hybrid population related to polar bears. Sykes proposed that a previously unrecognised bear population might explain some yeti reports. Importantly, the study did not claim to have found a yeti. It identified the samples as belonging to bears rather than primates, but suggested that the bears themselves might be genetically unusual.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.scmp.com/news/world/article/1334769/bryan-sykes-links-yeti-hair-samples-dna-ancient-polar-bear" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scmp.com">[South China Morning Post]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scmp.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">bryan sykes links yeti hair samples dna ancient polar bear</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">South China Morning PostBryan Sykes links yeti hair samples to the DNA of an...19 Oct 2013 — Sykes compared DNA from hair samples taken...</span></span></span>

Other geneticists quickly challenged this interpretation. Critics argued that the analysed DNA fragment was extremely short—roughly one hundred base pairs—and therefore insufficient for strong conclusions about bear evolution. ## What the study changed in cryptid testing

The lasting importance of the Oxford–Lausanne study is methodological rather than sensational. It showed that biological evidence associated with cryptids can be collected, screened and genetically identified using standard laboratory techniques. The project also highlighted the limits of such evidence. Hair samples without roots often contain only fragmented mitochondrial DNA, making precise identification difficult and sometimes leaving room for competing interpretations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4100498/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Genetic analysis of hair samples attributed to yeti, bigfoot and...by BC Sykes · 2014 · Cited by 49 — We have used rigorous decontami...</span></span></span>

Equally important, the study demonstrated the value of examining many samples rather than focusing on a single celebrated specimen. By treating cryptid evidence as a population of testable objects, the researchers were able to replace speculation with species-level identifications in most cases.
