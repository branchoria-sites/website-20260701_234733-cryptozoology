## Introduction

The Bermuda petrel, or cahow, is one of the clearest examples of a real-world “Lazarus” species: an animal believed extinct for centuries that was later found alive. What makes the cahow especially significant is not merely its rediscovery but what happened afterwards. In 1951, researchers located just 18 surviving breeding pairs on tiny offshore islets in Bermuda. That discovery transformed the species from a historical mystery into a long-term conservation challenge. Rather than proving that lost species routinely survive unnoticed, the cahow demonstrated something more specific and more valuable: a tiny remnant population can persist, but recovery requires decades of intensive management and protection.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bermuda_petrel" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia+2BirdLife DataZone]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Bermuda petrel</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Bermuda petrel</span></span></span><img src="{{"/assets/images/cryptozoology_99365b_lazarus_taxa_e4a778_cahow_recovery_ce257f-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Cahow illustration 1" data-theme-src-dark="{{"/assets/images/cryptozoology_99365b_lazarus_taxa_e4a778_cahow_recovery_ce257f-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{"/assets/images/cryptozoology_99365b_lazarus_taxa_e4a778_cahow_recovery_ce257f-Illustration-1-light.svg" | relative_url }}" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">
Within discussions of [cryptozoology]({{'cryptozoology/' | relative_url }}) and [Lazarus taxa]({{'lazarus-taxa/' | relative_url }}), the cahow stands as a reminder that confirmed rediscoveries depend on physical evidence, repeated monitoring and sustained scientific work—not on unverified survival rumours.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bermuda_petrel" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Bermuda petrel</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Bermuda petrel</span></span></span>

## The 1951 breeding-pair discovery

The decisive breakthrough came on 28 January 1951. American ornithologist Robert Cushman Murphy and Bermudian naturalist Louis Mowbray located surviving nesting birds on remote rocky islets in Castle Harbour. Accompanying them was a teenage Bermudian, David B. Wingate, who would later become the central figure in the species' recovery.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bermuda_petrel" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia+2The Nonsuch Expeditions]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Bermuda petrel</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Bermuda petrel</span></span></span>

The numbers were alarming. Only 18 breeding pairs were found, scattered across a handful of tiny islets. The entire known nesting area amounted to roughly one hectare of habitat. The population was producing only a small number of chicks each year, leaving little margin for further losses.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://datazone.birdlife.org/species/factsheet/bermuda-petrel-pterodroma-cahow" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: datazone.birdlife.org">[BirdLife DataZone+2National Wildlife Federation]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">datazone.birdlife.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">BirdLife DataZoneBermuda Petrel Pterodroma cahow Species FactsheetIn 1951, 18 pairs were rediscovered breeding on suboptimal rocky islets...</span></span></span>

This is why the cahow became such an important Lazarus-species case. Rediscovery did not reveal a hidden thriving population. Instead, it exposed a species balanced on the edge of extinction. The question immediately became whether 18 breeding pairs were enough to save a species.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bermuda_petrel" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Bermuda petrel</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Bermuda petrel</span></span></span>

## Why rediscovery alone was not enough

Finding the birds solved one mystery but revealed several new problems. The surviving colonies occupied extremely vulnerable nesting sites exposed to storms, erosion and flooding. The population's small size increased the risk that a single disaster could erase decades of survival. Introduced predators remained a constant concern. In fact, rats later eliminated one nesting colony despite ongoing protection efforts.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/259419612_Breeding_biology_and_population_increase_of_the_Endangered_Bermuda_Petrel_Pterodroma_cahow" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">18 pairs, dispersed among five small islets.... breeding phenology. The entire breeding population of Bermuda Petrel was monitored over...</span></span></span>

The species' biology also slowed recovery. Cahows breed late, produce only one egg per year, and spend much of their lives at sea. Even under favourable [conditions]({{'conditions/' | relative_url }}), population growth would be gradual. Conservationists could not rely on natural recovery alone.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bermuda_petrel" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Bermuda petrel</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Bermuda petrel</span></span></span>

As later observers noted, conservation biology itself was still a young discipline when the rediscovery occurred. There was no established playbook for rescuing a seabird species from such a precarious starting point.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.audubon.org/news/review-rare-birds-extraordinary-tale-bermuda-petrel-and-man-who-brought-it-back" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: audubon.org">[Audubon]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">audubon.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Review: Rare Birds, The Extraordinary Tale of the Bermuda</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Review: Rare Birds, The Extraordinary Tale of the Bermuda...May 17, 2013 — When the birds were found in 1951, there was no preced...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 17, 2013</span></span></span><img src="{{"/assets/images/cryptozoology_99365b_lazarus_taxa_e4a778_cahow_recovery_ce257f-Illustration-2-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Cahow illustration 2" data-theme-src-dark="{{"/assets/images/cryptozoology_99365b_lazarus_taxa_e4a778_cahow_recovery_ce257f-Illustration-2-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{"/assets/images/cryptozoology_99365b_lazarus_taxa_e4a778_cahow_recovery_ce257f-Illustration-2-light.svg" | relative_url }}" loading="lazy" decoding="async" fetchpriority="low">
## Management after rediscovery

The recovery programme that followed became one of the longest-running species-restoration efforts in the Atlantic.

### Rebuilding habitat on Nonsuch Island

David Wingate's most ambitious idea was to create a safer future breeding area on Nonsuch Island. Habitat restoration began decades before the site could support a new colony. Native vegetation was replanted and ecological conditions were gradually reconstructed to resemble Bermuda's original environment. Thousands of native and endemic plants were introduced as part of the project.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bermuda_petrel" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Bermuda petrel</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Bermuda petrel</span></span></span>

The goal was not simply to protect the remaining birds but to create room for expansion beyond the fragile original islets.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bermuda_petrel" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Bermuda petrel</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Bermuda petrel</span></span></span>

### Translocation and colony creation

One of the most innovative phases of recovery involved moving near-fledged chicks from the original colonies to artificial burrows on Nonsuch Island. Beginning in 2004, conservationists transferred young birds before they left for the sea, then hand-fed and monitored them until departure. The expectation was that these birds would later return to Nonsuch to breed, establishing a new colony.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bermuda_petrel" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia+2Audubon]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Bermuda petrel</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Bermuda petrel</span></span></span>

The programme achieved remarkably high fledging success and eventually helped establish breeding activity on the restored island. This reduced dependence on the tiny historic nesting sites and provided a buffer against future storm damage and sea-level rise.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bermuda_petrel" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Bermuda petrel</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Bermuda petrel</span></span></span><img src="{{"/assets/images/cryptozoology_99365b_lazarus_taxa_e4a778_cahow_recovery_ce257f-Illustration-3-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Cahow illustration 3" data-theme-src-dark="{{"/assets/images/cryptozoology_99365b_lazarus_taxa_e4a778_cahow_recovery_ce257f-Illustration-3-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{"/assets/images/cryptozoology_99365b_lazarus_taxa_e4a778_cahow_recovery_ce257f-Illustration-3-light.svg" | relative_url }}" loading="lazy" decoding="async" fetchpriority="low">
## Can 18 breeding pairs save a species?

The answer, in the cahow's case, appears to be yes—but only with extraordinary human assistance.
