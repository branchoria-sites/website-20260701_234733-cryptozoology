## Introduction

The Lord Howe Island stick insect is one of the strongest examples of a Lazarus taxon becoming more credible through modern genetics. Thought extinct after invasive rats devastated its native island in the early twentieth century, the insect appeared to survive in a tiny refuge on Ball’s Pyramid, a steep volcanic sea stack in the Tasman Sea. The rediscovery itself was remarkable, but an important question remained: were the insects found on Ball’s Pyramid really the same species that had vanished from Lord Howe Island, or a different but related insect? Modern DNA analysis of museum [specimens]({{'specimens/' | relative_url }}) finally provided the answer. By comparing genetic material from historical collections with living insects, researchers confirmed that the Ball’s Pyramid population was indeed the surviving Lord Howe Island stick insect, turning a dramatic rediscovery into a scientifically verified survival story.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/320329030_Museum_Genomics_Confirms_that_the_Lord_Howe_Island_Stick_Insect_Survived_Extinction" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate+2csiro.au]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">2017) found minimal genetic differentiation (&lt; 1%) between specimens of the stick insect Dryococelus australis from Ball&#x27;s...Read more...</span></span></span><img src="{{"/assets/images/cryptozoology_99365b_lazarus_taxa_e4a778_stick_insect_dna_9a7778-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Stick Insect illustration 1" data-theme-src-dark="{{"/assets/images/cryptozoology_99365b_lazarus_taxa_e4a778_stick_insect_dna_9a7778-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{"/assets/images/cryptozoology_99365b_lazarus_taxa_e4a778_stick_insect_dna_9a7778-Illustration-1-light.svg" | relative_url }}" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

## Why the Rediscovered Insects Looked Different

One reason DNA evidence became so important was that the Ball’s Pyramid insects did not perfectly match historical descriptions from Lord Howe Island.

Researchers noted that the rediscovered insects appeared stockier than museum specimens from the original population. Males also possessed more prominent leg spines. Such differences raised a legitimate possibility that the Ball’s Pyramid insects represented a separate species that had simply been mistaken for the supposedly extinct Lord Howe Island stick insect.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wired.com/story/welcome-back-lord-howe-island-stick-insect/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wired.com">[WIRED]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wired.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Triumphant Return of the Lord Howe Island Stick Insect5 Oct 2017 — The Ball&#x27;s Pyramid bugs were considerably stockier than ones...</span></span></span>

Morphological differences alone can be difficult to interpret. Isolated [populations]({{'populations/' | relative_url }}) often change in appearance because of environmental pressures, genetic drift, founder effects, or adaptation to unusual habitats. Ball’s Pyramid is an extreme environment compared with the forests of Lord Howe Island, and a population surviving there for many generations might be expected to diverge in body form without becoming a separate species.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wired.com/story/welcome-back-lord-howe-island-stick-insect" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wired.com">[WIRED]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wired.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">welcome back lord howe island stick insect</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Thought extinct since invasive rats devastated their population on Lord Howe Island in 1918, the insects’ rediscovery raised questions ab...</span></span></span>

For [cryptozoology]({{'cryptozoology/' | relative_url }}) and rediscovery claims more broadly, this is a recurring problem. Eyewitness observations or visual similarities can suggest an identification, but unusual appearance often fuels doubt. The Lord Howe case shows why physical resemblance alone is not always enough to settle the issue.<img src="{{"/assets/images/cryptozoology_99365b_lazarus_taxa_e4a778_stick_insect_dna_9a7778-Illustration-2-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Stick Insect illustration 2" data-theme-src-dark="{{"/assets/images/cryptozoology_99365b_lazarus_taxa_e4a778_stick_insect_dna_9a7778-Illustration-2-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{"/assets/images/cryptozoology_99365b_lazarus_taxa_e4a778_stick_insect_dna_9a7778-Illustration-2-light.svg" | relative_url }}" loading="lazy" decoding="async" fetchpriority="low">

## Why This Case Matters for Rediscovery Claims

Many alleged rediscoveries begin with circumstantial evidence: unusual sightings, isolated populations, or specimens that resemble a supposedly extinct animal. The Lord Howe Island stick insect demonstrates how those claims become substantially stronger when historical material exists for direct comparison.

Several features make this case especially important:<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--comparison" markdown="1">

* The extinct population was represented by preserved museum specimens.
* A living population was discovered in a plausible refuge.
* Researchers could compare historical and modern DNA directly.
* Genetic results agreed with earlier morphological assessments despite visible differences.
* The conclusion was strong enough to guide conservation planning and reintroduction efforts.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/230017970_The_recovery_programme_for_the_Lord_Howe_Island_Phasmid_Dryococelus_australis_following_its_rediscovery" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate+2ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ResearchGate(PDF) The recovery programme for the Lord Howe Island...24 Jan 2026 — Recent genetic studies confirmed that the stick insect...</span></span></span></div>

The case therefore serves as a model for evidence-based rediscovery.
