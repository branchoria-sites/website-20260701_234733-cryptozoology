## Introduction

Environmental DNA (eDNA) is often presented as a powerful way to test lake-monster claims, but its success depends heavily on where, when and how researchers collect [samples]({{ 'samples/' | relative_url }}). A lake survey does not simply ask whether unusual DNA is present. It asks whether the sampling design has a realistic chance of encountering the genetic traces of whatever is being sought. In a large lake, shoreline water, deep basins, river inflows and open water can contain very different biological signals. If a hypothetical large unknown animal spends most of its time in one part of the lake while researchers sample another, the survey may fail to detect it even if eDNA technology itself is working properly. For [cryptozoology]({{ 'cryptozoology/' | relative_url }}), sampling design is therefore not a technical detail; it is one of the main factors determining what conclusions a monster search can reasonably support. <span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10498028/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central+2naturemetrics.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">eDNA transport in inflows...Read more...</span></span></span>

## Why Location and Depth Matter

Large lakes are not biologically uniform. Different species occupy different habitats, and the DNA they shed often reflects those patterns. Research on lake eDNA has shown that vertical stratification—the layering of water at different temperatures—can influence where genetic material accumulates and how it moves through the water column. During stratified periods, DNA associated with species living in deeper water may remain concentrated at depth rather than mixing evenly throughout the lake. <span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2020.04.21.042820v1.full-text" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: biorxiv.org">[BioRxiv+2Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">biorxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Bio Rxiv Thermal stratification and fish thermal preference explain vertical</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Thermal stratification and fish thermal preference explain vertical...April 26, 2020 — Here, we tested how seasonal variation in...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 26, 2020</span></span></span>

For a lake-monster investigation, this matters because many proposed creatures are imagined as deep-water inhabitants. A survey that collects only shoreline or surface samples may provide an incomplete picture of the deepest habitats. Conversely, a survey focused exclusively on deep water could miss DNA signals concentrated near shorelines, wetlands or feeding areas. The practical question is not simply whether samples were collected, but whether they were collected from habitats that match the claimed behaviour of the target animal. naturemetrics.com+2U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service <span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.naturemetrics.com/kit-instructions/advice-note-sampling-strategies-for-aquatic-environments" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: naturemetrics.com">[naturemetrics.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">naturemetrics.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">sampling strategies for aquatic environments</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Nature MetricsAs in lakes, vertical stratification of the water (as a result of thermoclines) restricts mixing of eDNA, meaning that wate...</span></span></span>

The Loch Ness eDNA project illustrates this principle. Researchers collected roughly 250 water samples from around the loch, including shoreline locations, central waters and deep-water sites extending into the loch's deepest regions. ## How Water Movement Spreads DNA

A common assumption is that lake water mixes thoroughly, allowing a sample from one [location]({{ 'location/' | relative_url }}) to represent the entire lake. In reality, water movement can both help and hinder detection.

Studies of freshwater eDNA have shown that inflowing streams can transport DNA from upstream environments into lakes, while outflows can carry DNA away. Researchers studying connected lake systems found that inflow and outflow samples often contain signals that differ from those collected within the lake itself. In other words, DNA found near a river mouth may partly reflect organisms living elsewhere in the watershed rather than within the lake proper. <span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10498028/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central+2IISD]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">eDNA transport in inflows...Read more...</span></span></span>

This creates an important challenge for cryptozoological surveys. Imagine an unusual DNA sequence appearing near a major inflow. Before treating it as evidence of an unknown lake inhabitant, investigators would need to determine whether the material originated upstream and was merely transported into the lake. Likewise, the absence of DNA in one area may reflect dilution, transport or degradation rather than genuine absence of the organism. Hydrology becomes part of the interpretation. <span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cea.hal.science/cea-04664459/file/WIREs%20Water%20-%202024%20-%20URycki%20-%20A%20new%20flow%20path%20eDNA%20connecting%20hydrology%20and%20biology.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cea.hal.science">[CEA Hal+2Nature]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cea.hal.science</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">A new flow path: eDNA connecting hydrology and biologyby DR Urycki · 2024 · Cited by 25 — The hydrological cycle begins with preci...</span></span></span>

Water movement can also create detection hotspots. Areas where currents converge, where organisms congregate, or where biological material accumulates may produce stronger signals than nearby locations. A survey that relies on only a handful of collection points risks mistaking these local patterns for lake-wide reality. <span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.kmae-journal.org/articles/kmae/full_html/2026/01/kmae250141/kmae250141.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kmae-journal.org">[KMAE Journal+2MBMG Pensoft]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kmae-journal.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Integrated sampling</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">KMAE JournalEnvironmental DNA (eDNA) sampling strategies influence estimates...We compared point and spatially integrated sampling, two...</span></span></span>

## Why Timing Can Matter as Much as Geography

Sampling design is not only about place; it is also about time. Environmental DNA does not remain detectable indefinitely. Genetic traces degrade, settle into sediments or become diluted, [meaning]({{ 'meaning/' | relative_url }}) that samples generally provide information about relatively recent biological activity rather than a permanent record of everything that has ever lived in a lake. <span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.livescience.com/loch-ness-monster-dna-study.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: livescience.com">[Live Science+2NOAA Ocean Exploration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">livescience.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">loch ness monster dna study</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Live ScienceLoch Ness Contains No &#x27;Monster&#x27;&#x27; DNA, Say Scientists9 Sept 2019 — The scientific team surveyed Loch Ness in June 2018, taking...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 2018</span></span></span>

Seasonal lake [conditions]({{ 'conditions/' | relative_url }}) can alter DNA distribution. During periods of strong thermal stratification, surface and deep waters may exchange less material. During seasonal turnover, when lakes mix more thoroughly, DNA from different depths can become redistributed. An organism that is easier to detect in one season may be harder to detect in another simply because lake physics have changed. <span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2020.04.21.042820v1.full-text" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: biorxiv.org">[BioRxiv+2Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">biorxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Bio Rxiv Thermal stratification and fish thermal preference explain vertical</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Thermal stratification and fish thermal preference explain vertical...April 26, 2020 — Here, we tested how seasonal variation in...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 26, 2020</span></span></span>

For monster searches, a single survey conducted over a short period therefore provides a snapshot rather than a definitive census. Repeated sampling across seasons offers stronger evidence because it reduces the chance that unusual conditions during one sampling window shaped the result. <span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://mbmg.pensoft.net/article/104655/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mbmg.pensoft.net">[MBMG Pensoft]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mbmg.pensoft.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on pensoft.net.</span></span></span>

## What a Stronger Lake Survey Would Include

If the goal is to maximise the chance of detecting an unknown large aquatic animal, several sampling-design principles emerge from eDNA research.

**Multiple habitat types.** Samples should be collected from shorelines, open water, deep basins, shallow bays and major inflows rather than concentrating on a single environment. Different habitats can carry distinct DNA signatures. <span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.naturemetrics.com/kit-instructions/advice-note-sampling-strategies-for-aquatic-environments" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: naturemetrics.com">[naturemetrics.com+2PubMed Central]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">naturemetrics.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">sampling strategies for aquatic environments</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Nature MetricsAs in lakes, vertical stratification of the water (as a result of thermoclines) restricts mixing of eDNA, meaning that wate...</span></span></span>

## The Main Lesson for Monster Searches

The most important insight from modern eDNA work is that a lake is not a single sampling unit. It is a collection of interconnected habitats linked by depth, currents, inflows and seasonal mixing. A negative result from a poorly designed survey may say little about whether an unusual organism exists. Conversely, a well-designed survey that systematically samples multiple depths, locations and seasons can place much stronger limits on what is likely to inhabit a lake.

For cryptozoology, this means that the quality of the sampling plan is often as important as the genetic technology itself. The question is not merely whether researchers looked for monster DNA, but whether they looked in the places where such DNA would most likely be found. <span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10498028/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central+2naturemetrics.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">eDNA transport in inflows...Read more...</span></span></span>
